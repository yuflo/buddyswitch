# Evidence Rubric

Use this rubric to rank evidence quality.

## Strong evidence

Evidence is strong when it is:
- **concrete**: describes a real workflow, event, or cost
- **repeated**: appears across multiple independent sources
- **recent**: likely still relevant to the current market
- **behavior-linked**: shows what users actually did, paid for, or hacked together
- **segment-specific**: clearly ties to a type of user or team

Examples:
- users repeatedly describe the same manual workaround
- reviewers complain about the same switching trigger
- users say they pay for two tools because neither solves the whole problem
- a team explains exactly how much time or revenue is lost

## Medium evidence

Evidence is medium when it is:
- plausible and specific, but isolated
- based on a single thread or review cluster
- partially inferred from credible sources
- descriptive of a pain without a clear cost or commitment signal

Examples:
- one strong complaint with detailed context
- one niche community discussing the problem repeatedly but without payment clues
- a competitor changelog suggesting a common demand area

## Weak evidence

Evidence is weak when it is:
- purely aspirational or hypothetical
- vague praise without cost or urgency
- marketing-led rather than user-led
- old and not validated against newer signals
- too broad to tie to a useful segment

Examples:
- “this would be cool”
- “I might use this someday”
- generic lists of trends without real user behavior
- homepage claims with no corroborating user evidence

## Commitment signals

Give extra weight to signs that users are willing to commit.

### Highest-value signals
- paying for an existing solution
- stitching multiple tools together to solve the problem
- maintaining internal tooling or spreadsheets to cope
- asking for demos, pilots, or integrations
- leaving waitlist emails with context
- agreeing to interviews, pilots, or manual-service experiments

### Medium-value signals
- active switching consideration
- repeated feature requests
- detailed complaints with clear stakes
- public comparisons among alternatives

### Low-value signals
- likes, applause, generic praise
- “interesting idea” comments
- broad market trend language without specific behavior

## Segment sharpness test

A promising segment is one that can be described with:
- role or identity
- business context
- workflow context
- trigger moment
- current workaround

Bad segment:
- marketers

Better segment:
- solo founders running cold outbound for B2B SaaS without an SDR

## PMF confidence guidance

### Low confidence
- weak or scattered pain signals
- few repeated complaints
- unclear segment
- unclear alternatives
- no commitment signals

### Medium confidence
- repeated pain in a plausible segment
- clear alternatives with known weaknesses
- some cost or urgency signals
- next validation step is obvious

### High confidence
Desk research alone rarely justifies this.
Use only when:
- pain is strongly repeated across multiple sources
- alternatives are clearly unsatisfying
- segment is sharp
- there are credible commitment signals
- next steps are about execution, not basic problem validation
